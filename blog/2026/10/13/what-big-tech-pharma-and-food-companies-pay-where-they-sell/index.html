    What Big Tech, Pharma and Food Companies Pay Where They Sell

    Amazon's European retail entity booked 51.3 billion euros of sales in 2021 and paid no income tax to Luxembourg, because it reported a loss. That is all in the filings, and it is legal.

    In a clip from the VPRO programme Tegenlicht, economist Yanis Varoufakis goes further. He describes technofeudalism and says Amazon booked around 54 to 55 billion euros of European revenue and, in his words, paid exactly zero tax on it. Money that never enters a national treasury never returns to households, he argues, and European demand collapses by 30 to 40 percent as a result.

    The first claim can be checked against the documents. The second cannot. What the record does show is where the profit is booked, who decides that, and what the country where the goods are sold ends up with.

    Apple: a rate of 0.005 percent¶

    Start with the number that is hardest to believe. In 2011 Apple's subsidiary Apple Sales International recorded 22 billion dollars of profit. Under the tax rulings it operated on, roughly 50 million euros of that was considered taxable in Ireland, which left 15.95 billion euros untaxed. Apple paid less than 10 million euros in Irish corporate tax that year, an effective rate of about 0.05 percent on its total annual profit. By 2014 that rate had fallen to 0.005 percent, according to the European Commission.

    The Commission's conclusion in August 2016 went further than one company's rate. In its words, "the tax treatment in Ireland enabled Apple to avoid taxation on almost all profits generated by sales of Apple products in the entire EU Single Market". Ireland had granted undue tax benefits of up to 13 billion euros, and had to recover them.

    The case then took eight years. The General Court annulled the decision in July 2020 because the Commission had not proven a selective advantage to the required legal standard. On 10 September 2024 the Court of Justice set that judgment aside and confirmed the 2016 decision. Commissioner Vestager called it "a big win for European citizens and for tax justice".

    The money followed, slowly. Apple's public country-by-country report for fiscal 2025 lists 213.6 billion dollars of revenue in Ireland, 34.6 billion dollars of pre-tax profit and 5,575 employees. The 17.08 billion dollars paid to Ireland that year sits far above the 4.78 billion dollars of tax accrued. Apple explains the difference: escrowed funds were released to Ireland under the state aid decision, with the corresponding expense already taken in fiscal 2024.

    Amazon: the entity that reported a loss¶

    The numbers in the clip sit closest to Amazon EU Sarl. That entity books e-commerce for the UK, Germany, France, Italy, Spain, Poland, Sweden and the Netherlands in Luxembourg. It recorded 44 billion euros of sales in 2020 and 51.3 billion in 2021. In both years it reported a loss, 1.2 billion and 1.16 billion euros, and therefore paid no income tax to Luxembourg. Bloomberg reported from the filings that it also received one billion euros in tax credits in 2021.

    Brussels tried the same route here and lost it. In 2017 the Commission ordered Luxembourg to recover around 250 million euros of undue benefits. On 14 December 2023 the Court of Justice annulled that decision: the Commission had not established that the ruling was incompatible state aid.

    Amazon does not accept the broader reading. "Across Europe, we pay corporate tax amounting to hundreds of millions of euros," the company said in response to the Luxembourg reporting. Both statements can be true at once. The entity in the filings owed nothing, and the group says its European entities together pay hundreds of millions. The tax is real, and it lands somewhere other than where the sales happen. Under the rules that apply, the country where the customer clicks to buy has no claim on it.

    Pharma: 75 percent of the income is offshore¶

    The American evidence is blunt: a Senate committee had the Joint Committee on Taxation compute industry rates from SEC filings. Pharmaceutical companies went from an average worldwide effective rate of 19.6 percent in 2014 to 2016 down to 11.6 percent in 2019 and 2020. Their US domestic rate fell from 27.0 to 15.7 percent over the same period.

    Where the income is reported is the point. By those same figures, 75 percent of all Big Pharma income is reported offshore for tax purposes. For non-manufacturing companies the foreign share of taxable income is 22 percent; for non-pharma manufacturing it is 45 percent.

    In 2021 the effective rate of every one of the seven largest US pharmaceutical corporations was below 15 percent, well under the 21 percent statutory rate. Johnson & Johnson: 8.3 percent. Pfizer: 7.6 percent. Merck: 11 percent.

    Pfizer's own accounting is the clearest case. In 2019 it sold 20 billion dollars of drugs to US customers and reported zero dollars of taxable US profit, telling the IRS that 100 percent of its income was earned offshore. Its effective rate stayed between 5.3 percent and 9.6 percent over those years. Senator Ron Wyden, who commissioned the investigation, called it a scheme that "could be the largest tax-dodging scheme in the history of Big Pharma". Pfizer disputes that characterisation.

    AbbVie shows the mechanics. The company holds the intellectual property rights to Humira in a Bermuda subsidiary and manufactures in Puerto Rico. Because US tax law treats Puerto Rico as foreign, that income is taxed at the GILTI rate of 10.5 percent rather than 21 percent. A Senate report in 2022 found that AbbVie booked 99 percent of its taxable income offshore while generating 75 percent of its sales from US patients.

    The IRS is now pursuing Amgen over 24 billion dollars of income it says was shifted to Puerto Rico subsidiaries, seeking 10.7 billion dollars in back taxes and penalties. In 2021 Amgen generated 70 percent of its sales in the United States and reported 28 percent of its pretax income there.

    One caveat applies here. The Senate material is Democratic staff work that names specific companies. The data underneath comes from the companies' own 10-K filings; the interpretation and the framing are the committee's.

    Food: the cases that went the other way¶

    Food and drink is where the pattern breaks. Two cases did go against the companies here, and both were unwound on appeal.

    Luxembourg recovered 23.1 million euros from Fiat, and the Netherlands recovered 25.7 million euros from Starbucks, after the 2015 decisions. The General Court annulled the Starbucks decision in 2019 because the Commission could not prove that an advantage existed. In 2022 the Court of Justice annulled the Fiat decision because the Commission had applied the arm's length principle and the reference framework incorrectly. The same methodological problem ended the Engie case in 2023.

    The published rates for the sector sit at or above what the law asks. Coca-Cola reported an effective rate of 17.4 percent for 2023, AB InBev 25.2 percent, Nestlé 24.6 percent for 2025, Unilever 29 percent.

    Those rates do not settle the matter. Coca-Cola has been fighting the IRS for years over transfer pricing, and in November 2020 the US Tax Court predominantly sided with the IRS. Coca-Cola told investors that continuing the disputed methodology would raise its effective rate by about 3.5 percentage points.

    The narrowest case of all is McDonald's. In December 2015 the Commission opened an investigation into McDonald's Europe Franchising, which had paid no corporate tax in Luxembourg since 2009 despite profits above 250 million euros in 2013. Three years later it closed the case without a recovery, because the cause was a mismatch between Luxembourg and US tax laws rather than special treatment by Luxembourg.

    Then came the sentence that describes the whole field: "Of course, the fact remains that McDonald's did not pay any taxes on these profits, and this is not how it should be from a tax fairness point of view."

    What the new rules require¶

    Profit shifting is not primarily a matter of fraud. The European Parliament's research service calls transfer pricing the mispricing of resources or intellectual property, so that costs land in high-tax countries and revenues in low-tax ones. On shell entities, the Commission put it plainly: legal entities without minimal substance "continue to pose a risk of being used for improper tax purposes".

    Three rules now bite. The Pillar Two directive, adopted in December 2022, collects a top-up tax whenever a multinational group's effective rate in a jurisdiction falls below 15 percent. It applies to groups with at least 750 million euros of revenue, from fiscal years starting on or after 31 December 2023.

    The public country-by-country reporting directive requires large multinationals to disclose, per EU member state and per non-cooperative jurisdiction, how much tax they pay and where. It starts with the first financial year beginning on or after 22 June 2024. ATAD caps interest deductions at 30 percent of EBITDA and closes hybrid mismatch arrangements.

    The first public reports are arriving, and the EU Tax Observatory has found a pattern: a large group of companies filed reports covering a single member state, Romania, rather than the whole group. PepsiCo's Romanian subsidiaries told the register that they had asked the parent for the global data and that "PepsiCo Inc. did not make this information available to the subsidiaries". The register is filled in by the companies themselves, so the public's view of where the tax goes depends on the parties who choose where it goes.

    Estimates differ by method. The OECD puts the annual global cost of base erosion and profit shifting at 100 to 240 billion dollars, equal to 4 to 10 percent of global corporate income tax revenue. It adds one qualification: "although some BEPS schemes are illegal, most are not."

    The Commission estimates 35 to 70 billion euros lost each year in the EU, and the Tax Justice Network puts global losses at 492 billion dollars a year. The OECD also expects BEPS activity to fall by roughly half under a minimum tax, with countries collecting around 150 billion dollars more each year.

    The counter-move came in June 2025. The US Treasury secured a "side-by-side" arrangement under which American-parented groups are excluded from the two main Pillar Two rules. The carve-out leaves the rules in place and takes a large share of the tax base out of them.

    Where the clip's numbers drift¶

    The revenue figure in the clip does not match the record. The Luxembourg filings document 44 billion euros of European sales in 2020 and 51.3 billion in 2021. Neither is 54 or 55 billion, and the headline figure for 2021 circulated as dollars rather than euros.

    The "zero tax" claim is true for one entity and one tax. Amazon EU Sarl paid no income tax to Luxembourg in 2020 and 2021 because it reported losses. It is not true of the group's European tax bill as a whole, and Amazon says so itself.

    The claim that European demand collapses by 30 to 40 percent does not appear in any source I could find, primary or secondary. It is the analytical part of the argument rather than a measurement, and reading it as data would be a mistake.

    The two big cases make that last point for me. Apple's ended with a 13 billion euro recovery confirmed at the highest level. Amazon's ended with the Commission's decision annulled. The law asks whether a selective advantage was proven, not whether tax was paid, and those two questions produced opposite answers in two cases that look identical from the outside.
