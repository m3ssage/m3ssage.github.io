A guaranteed £10,000, or a one in four chance at £50,000. You play once, and if the gamble loses you walk away with nothing.

Around 80% of people take the guarantee. The gamble is worth £12,500 on average, so most of us accept a smaller expected win in exchange for never seeing zero.

James Shack, a UK financial planner who also studied psychology, opens his latest video with that choice. Fifteen minutes later the same reflex has been traced back to a room full of people who could retire and don't, still working for a number that stopped changing their life years ago.

### The gamble behind the certainty effect

The question is a variation on the problems Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky used in 1979 to build prospect theory. That work eventually put Kahneman at the front of the 2002 economics Nobel. He received it alone. Tversky had died in 1996, and the prize is not given posthumously.

Their experiments found that a large majority of people take a certain gain over a larger expected gain. They named the pattern the certainty effect.

Underneath it sits loss aversion. Their later work on reference-dependent choice found that losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains feel good. Seen that way, the safe £10,000 is also a way of not losing £10,000.

Shack gives the opposite reading airtime too. Refusing the gamble can be perfectly rational. If £10,000 clears a credit card and keeps the roof on, protecting it beats a 75% chance of nothing.

He then applies the same reflex to the decision that matters. Handing in notice feels like a certain loss of income. Staying on feels like keeping something you already have. The certainty effect and loss aversion do the rest, which is why people with enough money still cannot walk out of the building, and why the years go instead.

The sharper question is where on that ladder you would switch. Hold the ratio and raise the guaranteed amount from £10 to a million, and a point arrives where the certainty is the life you wanted anyway. Past that point the gamble asks you to risk that life for money you have no use for.

### Utility bends, it does not climb straight

Money's usefulness is not a straight line, which is why the ladder has a switch point at all.

Hand £10,000 to someone behind on rent and it is a lifeline. Give the same £10,000 to a billionaire and it disappears into the noise without a ripple.

Shack borrows the 1943 Maslow paper to give the curve a shape: physiological needs, safety, belonging, esteem, self-actualisation. Money is an efficient tool for the first three. Above them it degrades fast. A nicer car or a better holiday usually costs two or three times as much as the last one for a small gain, and hedonic adaptation quietly resets the baseline within months.

Now picture who is watching. Someone who saves, who understands compounding, who has read enough about pensions to sit through fifteen minutes on them. Housing is covered. Security is covered. Some freedom is covered. If something still feels missing, the flat part of the curve is exactly where that feeling shows up, and Shack's read is that the missing piece gets misdiagnosed as a missing figure.

His prediction for anyone who sets a target and waits is blunter than that. You reach the number in five or ten years, and the definition of enough has quietly moved with you. So you blow straight past it and keep working, chasing money that will not deliver the feeling you were actually after. The saving is done. The spending of it keeps getting postponed.

### Time is the asset that appreciates

An hour at 25 and an hour at 65 are not worth the same. Almost every long-range financial plan treats them as interchangeable.

Shack's illustration is Warren Buffett, in his mid-nineties with a fortune Forbes estimates in the region of $150 billion. Ask what he would trade for one more healthy year and the honest answer is most of the pile. Everything else on that balance sheet has a buyer. The last item never does.

Health and time feed each other. The years in which money still converts into a sailing trip, a long walk or a holiday with friends are the same years the body cooperates. Staying fit in your fifties and sixties takes time, and a 50-hour desk week does not leave it.

Give that £10,000 to a fit man in his fifties and it becomes an active holiday, maybe with the people he has known for thirty years. Give the same money to a man in his eighties, when energy is failing and the friends who would have come along are gone, and it cannot convert into anything close to the same experience.

So the trade is bigger than the payslip suggests. You are not only spending present time on money. You may be spending the future time that would have made the money worth having.

Gifting runs into the same arithmetic. If the money is going to your children, the moment it would help most is probably their thirties, when it changes what they can do. By the time it arrives in their fifties or sixties, it moves very little. Spending, or giving, at the right moment can be worth more than holding on.

### Why 57 specifically

Two arguments carry the number, and only one of them is about psychology.

The first is physical. At 57 you still feel capable, and you are still fit enough to stay fit. That makes the window where wealth and health overlap unusually wide. Stepping out of full-time stress at that age may add years rather than take them.

The second is structural and specifically British. The normal minimum pension age rises from 55 to 57 on 6 April 2028. That date is when most people can first touch the largest financial asset they own. A protected pension age keeps 55 for rights held before 4 November 2021, and outside the UK the rulebook differs. The State Pension is a separate and later matter: 66 today, rising to 67 in April 2028.

Half of that overlap has an expiry date, and it is the half nobody invoices you for. The wealth keeps compounding for as long as you leave it alone. The hiking, the sailing and the long trips with old friends do not wait, and no later balance reopens them.

Shack's closing position is modest. Work is not the enemy. When the job supplies status, community and purpose, it is doing exactly what the top of the pyramid needs. The trouble is the other version, the one where you keep going for a figure that stopped buying satisfaction years earlier. From the outside those two look identical.

### What the video leaves out

The argument is psychological, and the video puts no numbers on the point where enough begins. No withdrawal rate. No sequence-of-returns risk, no longevity or inflation assumption. Shack points to a follow-up case study instead, which is reasonable for a 15-minute YouTube video and thin as a retirement plan.

That gap is where the risk actually sits for a viewer. Until someone writes down what they spend, what they owe and how long the money has to last, "probably enough" stays a guess. A guess is a comfortable thing to keep at the office. The video is clear that the arithmetic has to happen, and then leaves it to the follow-up.

It is also worth knowing who is talking. The video is issued by Shack Media on behalf of Nova Wealth, the FCA-regulated advice firm where Shack is a partner. The closing message is that you need to interrogate your own numbers, ideally with someone who does it professionally. That does not make the psychology wrong, and it does explain why the advice industry turns up in the conclusion.

Finally, 57 is a policy date rather than a discovery about human beings. The curve, the loss aversion and the shrinking value of an extra year apply at 45 and at 67 just as well. The date only decides when the pension wrapper stops being locked.
