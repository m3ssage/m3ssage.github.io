On 12 October 2026 the Dutch ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries, Food Security and Nature announced that "the permit system for zoos is being overhauled": zoos that are ahead should get room to innovate, while zoos that lag behind are to be pushed to develop. The underlying vision letter, signed by state secretary Silvio Erkens on 9 October, sets out a step-by-step revision of a system that has been in place since 2003.

The debate around that revision comes down to two readings of the same documents. One is that periodic review and output-based requirements finally give the permit system teeth. The other is that it mainly produces legal uncertainty and a cost base that favours large parks. This article sets out what the documents actually say, and where they are silent.

<!-- more -->

### What is actually changing

Zoo permits in the Netherlands implement the European zoo directive (1999/22/EC) through chapter 4.1 of the Besluit houders van dieren. Article 4.2(1) forbids operating a zoo without a ministerial permit; article 4.4 requires an inspection before a permit is granted; article 4.10 obliges the permit holder to contribute to species conservation; article 4.13 gives a zoo a reasonable period — at most two years — to repair a situation before a permit can be withdrawn. The licensing authority is RVO, which asks the Visitatiecommissie Dierentuinen for advice, and NVWA carries out inspections. RVO states that a normal application takes eight weeks, extendable to sixteen.

According to the vision letter, the plan is a *stelselwijziging*: first the permit system, partly to reduce regulatory burden, then the substantive themes — the letter lists eleven, including animal welfare, surplus animals, public interactions, education, conservation, veterinary care and safety. The instrument named in the letter is an amendment of chapter 4.1 of the Besluit houders van dieren; no draft decision or bill has been published yet.

### The diagnosis behind the revision

The critique does not come from animal-rights groups but from the government's own advisory council. In its own-initiative opinion *De toekomst is er zoo* (26 March 2025), the Raad voor Dieraangelegenheden (RDA) found that permits are issued for an indefinite period, so a zoo that changes nothing keeps its permit: only changes are re-assessed. "The consequence," the council wrote, "is that zoos are barely steered through the permit system towards new insights on animal welfare and the goals of zoos." It recommended considering a fixed periodic reassessment or a switch to permits for a limited term.

The RDA also found that the legal framework steers on intended effort rather than on output: policy protocols are input-oriented, so the actual impact zoos have on conservation and education differs widely. It flagged welfare risks for animals held behind the scenes, whose enclosures are often not species-specific and are not assessed at all in licensing, and it noted that NVWA works mainly on reports — which are less likely for animals that the public cannot see. The council also observed that a zoo permit has, to date, never been partly or wholly withdrawn.

The ministry's own letter repeats the diagnosis in sharper terms: because a zoo that does nothing keeps its outdated permit, and only renovating zoos are re-tested, "the regulatory burden is felt mainly by the zoos that want to move forward." Its stated aim is that front-runners are not hindered while laggards are steered to develop — possibly with self-regulation by the front-runners.

### Where the evidence gets thinner

Whether periodic review actually raises welfare is not something the documents demonstrate. The RDA's opinion is reasoning about incentives, not a measurement of welfare outcomes; the council explicitly notes that some open norms in the current framework — the veterinary protocol is the example given — lead to undesirable welfare risks, which suggests the design of the norms matters as much as their periodicity. The vision letter says the substantive themes still have to be worked out and calls the plan "an ambitious and sizeable system change".

Enforcement data are also not zoo-specific. NVWA's 2025 annual review reports 4,418 animal-welfare inspections at 3,136 unique businesses, up from 4,095 inspections in 2024, but the publication does not break that down for zoos. The Welfare Association (Dierenbescherming) wrote in March 2025 that not all species fare equally well in zoos, that some species clearly need improvement and others may not belong in a zoo at all, and that a general verdict is therefore difficult.

The most detailed recent enforcement file is the Dolfinarium, and it shows how hard the current rules are to apply. The state secretary imposed a last-under-dwangsom (a penalty order) on 28 March 2025 for breaching the Besluit houders van dieren and permit conditions. A preliminary-relief judge suspended it in May 2025 because the evidence was not convincing enough: both the state secretary and his expert had judged video fragments without taking the zoo's full educational programme into account. In September 2025 a second order was set aside as inadequately prepared and insufficiently reasoned, in breach of articles 3:2 and 7:12 of the General Administrative Law Act; the Dolfinarium had invoked legal certainty and legitimate expectations. On 6 October 2026 the College van Beroep voor het bedrijfsleven annulled two further penalty orders for lack of a "proper and verifiable establishment of the relevant facts", while rejecting the argument that the permit condition was too unclear to be enforceable.

### Legal uncertainty and the cost question

That case law is the most concrete material in the file on legal certainty, and it cuts both ways. The highest business court confirmed that enforcement must rest on verifiable facts and that sloppy preparation voids a decision — protection for permit holders, and a warning that a permit condition written in open terms is only as good as the evidence behind it. At the same time the court rejected the zoo's claim that the condition was insufficiently clear, so an open norm is not automatically unenforceable.

RVO itself acknowledges the procedural friction. Its page on a pilot with pre-consultation for enclosure construction, checked 1 October 2026, says a pre-meeting can give "more clarity about the sometimes complex application procedure for amending your permit". Since the pilot opened in January, two zoos had used it; there was room for seven more, and selection aims for a reflection of the sector including non-members of the two national associations.

Who would bear the costs of stricter, periodically tested permits is not measured in any of these documents. What the RDA does record is how unevenly the sector is built. Of the 59 institutions with a permit (its figure as of 1 March 2025; RVO's own list dated 4 August 2026 contains 60), thirteen are united in the NVD, which says its members receive some ten million visits a year, and fourteen are in Dier en Park, the association of smaller regional animal parks. Roughly half of all permitted zoos belong to neither. Around five zoos draw more than a million visitors a year, while some smaller ones welcome about 25,000. Some sit on more than 150 hectares; a butterfly house or aquarium may occupy a few hundred square metres. Several are part of larger groups: Libéma owns Safaripark Beekse Bergen, Eindhoven Zoo, Zoo Parc Overloon and AquaZoo Leeuwarden, and the Spanish Aspro Parks — with 68 leisure sites in ten countries — owns the Dolfinarium and Deltapark Neeltje Jans. In small zoos the owner is often director, manager and animal keeper at once.

The ministry's letter states the consequence of that structure plainly, in a sentence that is easy to read as a warning about the revision's own reach: the two associations represent about half of the sector and attract by far most visitors, but "the zoos on which changes to the regulations may have the greatest impact are not united".

### What is not settled

The revision is at the stage of intentions. There is no draft amendment, no published term for permits, no definition of the "measurable educational contribution" the letter asks for, and no cost estimate for what a period of validity, periodic re-assessment or output conditions would mean for a small institution. The welfare effect of the change is asserted by design rather than evidenced by measurement, and the strongest available data on enforcement — the Dolfinarium cases — show decisions being annulled on procedure rather than resolved on the merits. Both readings of the file therefore survive it: a system that was never retested becomes one that is, or a set of open norms whose costs and clarity are settled in court, case by case, with the front-runners invited to help write the rules and the least-organised half of the sector watching from outside.
