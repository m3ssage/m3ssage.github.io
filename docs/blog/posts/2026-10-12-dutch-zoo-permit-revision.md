On 12 October 2026 the Dutch ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries, Food Security and Nature said it is overhauling the permit system for zoos. The aim as stated: give the zoos that are ahead room to innovate, and push the ones that lag behind to develop. The vision letter behind that announcement was signed by state secretary Silvio Erkens on 9 October, and it proposes to rewrite a system that has stood since 2003.

Two readings of the same file are doing the rounds. One says periodic review and output-based requirements finally give the permit system teeth. The other says it produces legal uncertainty and a cost base that suits big parks. I went through the documents to see which reading holds up. Both do, and that is the interesting part.

<!-- more -->

### What is actually changing

Zoo permits in the Netherlands implement the European zoo directive (1999/22/EC) through chapter 4.1 of the Besluit houders van dieren. Article 4.2(1) forbids operating a zoo without a ministerial permit. Article 4.4 requires an inspection before the permit is granted. Article 4.10 obliges the permit holder to contribute to species conservation, and article 4.13 gives a zoo a reasonable period, at most two years, to repair a situation before the permit can be withdrawn. RVO issues the permits and asks the Visitatiecommissie Dierentuinen for advice. NVWA carries out the inspections. A normal application takes eight weeks, extendable to sixteen, according to RVO.

The plan is a stelselwijziging. First the permit system, partly to cut regulatory burden, then the substantive themes. The letter lists eleven of them, including animal welfare, surplus animals, public interactions, education, conservation, veterinary care and safety. The instrument named in the letter is an amendment of chapter 4.1 of the Besluit houders van dieren. No draft decision or bill has appeared yet.

### Why the ministry wants to change it

The sharpest critique in the file comes from the government's own advisory council rather than from animal-rights groups. In its own-initiative opinion De toekomst is er zoo (26 March 2025), the Raad voor Dieraangelegenheden found that permits are issued for an indefinite period. A zoo that changes nothing keeps its permit, because only changes are re-assessed. "The consequence," the council wrote, "is that zoos are barely steered through the permit system towards new insights on animal welfare and the goals of zoos." It suggested a fixed periodic reassessment, or a switch to permits for a limited term.

The council also found that the framework steers on intended effort rather than on output. Policy protocols are input-oriented, so the actual impact zoos have on conservation and education varies widely. It flagged welfare risks for animals held behind the scenes, whose enclosures are often not species-specific and are not assessed at all in licensing. NVWA works mainly on reports, and reports are less likely for animals the public cannot see. One line in the opinion is easy to miss: a zoo permit has, to date, never been partly or wholly withdrawn.

The ministry's own letter repeats the diagnosis in blunter terms. A zoo that does nothing keeps its outdated permit, and only renovating zoos are re-tested, so "the regulatory burden is felt mainly by the zoos that want to move forward." The stated aim is that front-runners are not hindered while laggards are steered to develop, possibly with self-regulation by the front-runners.

### Where the evidence gets thinner

That periodic review raises welfare is an assumption in these documents, not a finding. The RDA reasons about incentives. It does flag a design problem in the current rules: some open norms, the veterinary protocol being the example given, lead to undesirable welfare risks. So the content of the norms may matter more than how often they are checked. The vision letter says the substantive themes still have to be worked out and calls the whole thing "an ambitious and sizeable system change".

Enforcement figures are not zoo-specific either. NVWA's 2025 annual review reports 4,418 animal-welfare inspections at 3,136 unique businesses, up from 4,095 inspections in 2024, without a breakdown for zoos. The Dierenbescherming wrote in March 2025 that not all species fare equally well in zoos, that some species clearly need improvement and others may not belong in a zoo at all, and that this makes a general verdict difficult.

The most detailed recent enforcement file is the Dolfinarium, and it shows how hard the current rules are to apply. The state secretary imposed a last onder dwangsom on 28 March 2025 for breaching the Besluit houders van dieren and permit conditions. A preliminary-relief judge suspended it in May 2025, because the evidence was not convincing enough: both the state secretary and his expert had judged video fragments without taking the zoo's full educational programme into account. In September 2025 a second order was set aside as inadequately prepared and insufficiently reasoned, in breach of articles 3:2 and 7:12 of the General Administrative Law Act. The Dolfinarium had argued legal certainty and legitimate expectations. On 6 October 2026 the College van Beroep voor het bedrijfsleven annulled two further penalty orders for lack of a "proper and verifiable establishment of the facts", while rejecting the argument that the permit condition was too unclear to be enforceable.

### What the courts and the regulator show

That case law is the most concrete material in the file on legal certainty, and it cuts both ways. The highest business court confirmed that enforcement needs verifiable facts and that sloppy preparation voids a decision. That protects permit holders. It is also a warning that an open norm is only as good as the evidence behind it. The court did reject the claim that the condition was insufficiently clear, so an open norm is not automatically unenforceable.

RVO acknowledges the friction itself. Its page on a pilot with pre-consultation for enclosure construction, checked on 1 October 2026, says a pre-meeting can give "more clarity about the sometimes complex application procedure for amending your permit". Two zoos had used the pilot since it opened in January. There was room for seven more, and the selection aims for a reflection of the sector, including non-members of the two national associations.

### Who pays

No document here measures who bears the cost of stricter, periodically tested permits. The RDA does record how unevenly the sector is built. Of the 59 institutions with a permit as of 1 March 2025 (RVO's own list of 4 August 2026 has 60), thirteen are in the NVD, which says its members receive some ten million visits a year, and fourteen are in Dier en Park, the association of smaller regional animal parks. Roughly half of all permitted zoos belong to neither. About five zoos draw more than a million visitors a year, while some smaller ones welcome around 25,000. Some sit on more than 150 hectares; a butterfly house or aquarium may occupy a few hundred square metres. Several belong to larger groups: Libéma owns Safaripark Beekse Bergen, Eindhoven Zoo, Zoo Parc Overloon and AquaZoo Leeuwarden, and the Spanish Aspro Parks, with 68 leisure sites in ten countries, owns the Dolfinarium and Deltapark Neeltje Jans. In small zoos the owner is often director, manager and animal keeper at once.

The ministry knows this. Its letter notes that the two associations represent about half of the sector and attract by far most visitors, and then says that "the zoos on which changes to the regulations may have the greatest impact are not united".

### No draft, no term, no number

There is no draft amendment, no published term for permits, no definition of the "measurable educational contribution" the letter asks for, and no cost estimate for what a period of validity or periodic re-assessment would mean for a small institution. The welfare gain is asserted by design rather than measured. The best available enforcement material, the Dolfinarium cases, is a run of annulments on procedure rather than decisions on the merits.

Both readings survive the file, then. You can read it as a system that was never retested becoming one that is, or as a set of open norms whose costs and clarity get settled in court, case by case, while the front-runners help write the rules and the least-organised half of the sector watches from outside.
