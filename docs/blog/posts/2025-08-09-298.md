In a webinar published on the AnswerThis channel, co-founder and CMO Ryan McCarroll demonstrates an AI research assistant that searches the literature, drafts citation-backed text and summarises papers. The pitch is speed: literature work that took weeks, in minutes. That claim comes from the vendor, so this post sets it next to what universities and publishers currently say about AI-generated references — because the documentation on that side is unusually concrete.

<!-- more -->

### What the tool claims to do

According to the company's own description, AnswerThis is "an all-in-one AI research assistant that supports your entire workflow, from finding research gaps and collecting papers to summarizing, analyzing, and drafting citation-backed content for your research paper, dissertation, or thesis". In the demo the emphasis is on finding gaps in the literature, mapping citations between papers, and producing a first draft of a literature review with references attached — [the company says](https://answerthis.io/) that work which used to take months now takes minutes, and the Y Combinator profile describes the platform as used by [over 200,000 researchers](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/answerthis).

It is a paid product: the pricing page lists a free tier, a Pro tier at 35 dollars per seat per month and institutional licences, with annual-billing tiers from 0 to 100 dollars.

Two caveats belong in the same paragraph as the claims. First, the numbers on the vendor's own pages do not agree with each other — the homepage speaks of "1 Million+ researchers" and a "300M+ paper" database, the pricing FAQ mentions 250M+ papers and the Y Combinator listing says over 200,000 researchers. Second, a dedicated citation-map feature, a benchmark against Google Scholar or PubMed, and the "more than 20 citations in minutes" figure could not be confirmed outside the vendor's own material, so they are reported here as company claims rather than as measured results.

### The risk side is documented

The reason this matters more in research than in most other workflows is that a fabricated reference looks exactly like a real one. That has been measured:

- A peer-reviewed study in *Scientific Reports* (Walters & Wilder, 2023) examined 636 bibliographic citations in 84 AI-generated literature reviews and found that **55 percent of the GPT-3.5 citations and 18 percent of the GPT-4 citations were fabricated** — they did not correspond to real scholarly works. Of the real citations, 43 percent (GPT-3.5) and 24 percent (GPT-4) contained substantive errors.
- A study in the *Journal of Medical Internet Research* (Chelli et al., 2024) measured hallucinated references at 39.6 percent for GPT-3.5, 28.6 percent for GPT-4 and 91.4 percent for Bard when the models were asked to reproduce systematic reviews, and concluded that LLMs should not be used "as the primary or exclusive tool for conducting systematic reviews".

Publishers and universities have responded with rules rather than bans:

- **Authorship.** ICMJE's recommendations state that chatbots and other AI-assisted tools cannot be listed as authors, because they cannot take responsibility for accuracy, integrity and originality, and that authors must review and edit AI output as it "can be incorrect, incomplete, or biased". COPE's position statement reaches the same conclusion: AI tools "cannot meet the requirements for authorship".
- **Verification.** Erasmus University Rotterdam's guidelines tell staff and students *not* to use GenAI to look up or check facts, because a plausible answer is not a reliable one. Ghent University's research framework lists invented references and citations among the known limits of the tools; the University of Antwerp notes that AI-generated texts often lack proper sourcing or contain invented citations; Harvard's FAS guidance requires any use of generative AI to be acknowledged and cited, with the student bearing final responsibility for the output. The University of Amsterdam's policy states that GenAI output is not a reliable academic source.

None of that makes the tools useless — it fixes where the responsibility sits. A literature search that surfaces candidate papers is a different product from a system whose draft one submits.

### What to do with a tool like this

- Treat generated references as leads, not as sources: open every citation that goes into your bibliography, and confirm it exists with the DOI, journal and year.
- Keep the verification in the workflow, not after it: the studies above measured error rates in *bibliographies*, which is where an unnoticed fabrication becomes a plagiarism or integrity case.
- Check your institution's rules before you start, not before you submit: they differ on acknowledgment, permitted use and the responsibility clause.
- Ask for the audit trail. A tool that can show which papers a citation came from is materially different from one that only produces the sentence.
