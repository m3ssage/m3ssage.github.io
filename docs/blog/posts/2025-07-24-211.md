In episode #2010 of De Nieuwe Wereld, economist and columnist Jona van Loenen talks with Jelle van Baardewijk about the Dutch housing market. His position is that the shortage of houses is not what makes them expensive; the amount of money a buyer can borrow or bring along is. That claim sits next to the policy motto of the past decade — build, build, build — and it comes with proposals that would shift costs towards homeowners.

<!-- more -->

### The claim: financing capacity, not the shortage

Van Loenen's argument starts with a comparison that can be checked in the figures. In 2021 the estimated housing shortage fell from 331,000 to 279,000 homes, while house prices rose 15.2 percent that year — the largest increase in 21 years. If the shortage drove prices, he argues, those two numbers should not move in opposite directions. The mechanism he describes: the supply of homes barely responds to demand, so buyers compete mainly on how much they can pay, and how much they can pay follows from interest rates, incomes and lending standards.

He points to research of De Nederlandsche Bank (DNB) for support, and repeats that reference in a column in de Volkskrant (29 August 2024) and in his book on inheritance and wealth.

### What that DNB analysis actually says

The analysis Van Loenen refers to is *Financieringsruimte en huizenprijzen* (Eijsink & Van Dijk, DNB, 2023). Its conclusion: financing capacity is the most important driver of house prices, and other factors, such as new supply, are not significant in their model. A one-off increase of financing capacity by 3.5 percent translates into roughly 3.9 percent higher house prices after about 20 quarters — an elasticity close to 1. The analysis also notes that prices almost doubled between June 2013 and June 2022, driven mainly by interest rates (the average mortgage rate fell from 3.7 to about 1.7 percent) and by incomes (up more than 30 percent).

The same document adds a nuance that the short version of the argument leaves out. Building does not move prices in DNB's model, but the analysis states that structural construction *does* improve accessibility, meaning what a starter can actually afford. DNB's policy advice points the other way from stimulating demand: wider lending standards for starters are passed on one-to-one into higher prices, which is why the bank argues against relaxing them.

### The figures the conversation runs into

- **Prices.** Existing owner-occupied homes peaked in July 2022, became cheaper for roughly a year (in May 2023 they were 5.8 percent below the level of a year earlier), and have been rising again since June 2023. In 2025 existing homes were 8.6 percent more expensive than in 2024, and in December 2025 prices were 13.9 percent above the July 2022 peak, with an average sale price of almost 486,000 euros in the fourth quarter (CBS/Kadaster).
- **Shortage.** The Ministry of Housing (BZK) puts the statistical housing shortage for 2026 at 384,000 homes, or 4.6 percent of the stock, after 4.9 percent in 2024 and 4.8 percent in 2025. The ministry counts a shortage of around 2 percent as an acceptable level of tension; reaching that would take about 213,000 extra homes.
- **The pattern.** Prices fell when interest rates rose, in 2022-2023, and recovered while the shortage remained large. That combination is what makes the debate hard to settle with a single series: the two do not track each other one-to-one.

### The proposals he puts on the table

Van Loenen does not argue that building should stop. He argues that building alone will not make houses cheaper, and that the value that has accumulated in existing homes has to be taxed or written down. In the conversation and in his columns he lists a higher transfer tax, a tax on the overvalue (*overwaarde*) that sellers take to their next purchase, stricter limits on borrowing capacity, and a substantial increase in inheritance tax. He frames these as a correction of what he calls an unearned transfer of wealth between generations rather than as budget measures.

Each of those measures redistributes, and the conversation does not work out who absorbs the cost. DNB calculated in the same 2023 analysis that a 20 percent price drop would leave about 8 percent of mortgaged homes — roughly 300,000 households, mostly recent buyers — with a mortgage larger than the value of the house. Stricter lending limits reduce what starters can borrow first. A tax on housing wealth lands on the group that has already paid for the house, and the revenue would depend on rates that are still a political choice.

### What the conversation does not settle

This is a conversation, not a study: one guest, no counter-expert at the table, and the proposals are his own. What the available figures leave open is whether limiting financing capacity on its own would bring prices down, or mainly move the queue — fewer households that can buy, longer waiting lists in the rental sector — and how a deliberate price correction would work out for the roughly four million households with a mortgage. The conversation also stays away from the question of timing: Dutch prices have risen for eight consecutive quarters while the shortage slowly declined, which is the opposite pattern from the one both sides of the debate would predict from a single cause.
