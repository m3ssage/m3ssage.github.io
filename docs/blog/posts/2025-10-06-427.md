In 2022 Neom presented a city 170 kilometres long, 200 metres wide and 500 metres high. It was called The Line. Nine million people were supposed to live inside it, without cars or roads, on renewable power, at an official cost of around $500 billion.

The first phase now on the internal plans is 1.5 miles. At least $50 billion has already been spent, and the desert it stands in is marked by piling and deep trenches.

This post sets an architectural analysis of that design against the reporting on what has been built and paid for since. The analysis is Daniel Titchener's, from August 2025.

<!-- more -->

### The design that was announced

The published plans describe two parallel towers forming one continuous wall. The horizontal transport runs inside the structure rather than on the surface, so the city has no outside street at all.

Neom's own framing was that this layout would replace the outward growth of conventional cities with a structure the length of a country. Residents would reach services within a short walk. The drawings also include a marina, a stadium above it and a building suspended upside down from an arch. Reporting has questioned all three.

Those are the drawings. Titchener's question is a different one: can anyone live inside a wall?

### Titchener's argument

Titchener works through three themes, and all three are his reading of the design rather than findings about the construction site. The first is sprawl, inverted. Where a conventional city expands outward, The Line constrains everything along one axis. Internal high-speed transit then has to carry all movement. The enclosure leaves little room for the incremental expansion and change that cities normally absorb.

The second is liveability. Stand on an inner face and you lose daylight and ventilation. The varied street-level surroundings that an ordinary city provides are gone. Vertical farming and integrated services are offered as compensation. They are plans, not demonstrated practice.

The third is perfection. The structure is engineered from a fixed specification in one direction, which leaves little space for the adaptations a city acquires over decades. Titchener argues that the rigidity is a design risk in its own right.

Those are architectural objections. The reporting that follows is about money and schedule.

### The Financial Times

The Financial Times traced The Line through interviews with more than twenty people who worked on it. They were architects, engineers and former senior executives. The piece ran on 6 November 2025. It found that at least $50 billion has been spent. The desert is marked by piling and deep trenches, and the first phase has been scaled back sharply.

The same reporting gives the internal budget estimates. They moved from $1.6 trillion in late 2021 to around $4.5 trillion in the spring of 2022. Both figures describe the same project. The official estimate was around $500 billion. Only the smallest one was public.

### The Wall Street Journal

The Wall Street Journal reported that the first phase is now planned at roughly 1.5 miles of structure by 2030. That is 2.4 kilometres, and the approximately 10 miles previously envisioned is no longer the plan. Internal expectations for the first phase run below 200,000 residents. The announcement said nine million. A draft board presentation put the capital needed to build out Neom to its end state by 2080 at $8.8 trillion.

Neither the residents figure nor the capital figure appears in a published plan.

### CNBC

CNBC reported in July 2025 that the Public Investment Fund had engaged consulting firms for a strategic review of The Line's feasibility. The fund finances Neom. Neom confirmed the review and described such checks as common practice on long-term projects.

The fund holds the kingdom's own money. A shorter line therefore comes out of a national budget rather than out of a company's balance sheet. The fund that pays for the project is now asking whether the project works.

### What Neom says

Neom told the Financial Times that The Line remains "a strategic priority". Neom said the project would ultimately "provide a new blueprint for humanity by changing the way people live", and called it a "multi-generational development of unprecedented scale and complexity". The company says attention has moved to "the complex engineering and detailed design work associated with the first phase".

Faisal Al Ibrahim, the Saudi minister of economy and planning, was asked about the shortened first phase in a CNBC interview. His reply was that "there is no change in scale — it is a long-term project that is modular in design". He added that the kingdom does not want to overheat its economy. Neom's deputy chief executive Rayan Fayez said the project's budget "evolves every day".

The internal plan has the first phase at 1.5 miles. None of those three answers contains a figure.

### The step not taken

No cancellation of The Line has been announced. Neom has not published a revised master plan, budget or timeline, so the figures above come from internal documents and from people involved rather than from official releases.

The step that would make the project hard to reverse is awarding the contracts for the construction above ground. According to the Financial Times, that step had not been taken. A shortened first phase could still be a staging post, or it could be the practical end of the 170-kilometre plan.

The completed groundwork is the other open question. Nobody has said what happens to it if the vertical building does not follow.
