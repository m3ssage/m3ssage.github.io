The final menstrual period is one of the most consequential days in a woman's endocrine life, and nobody writes it down. That missing date is why the timeline of menopause stayed wrong for a generation. Without it, every lab result is stamped with an age, and two women of 51 can be ten years apart in their transition.

A team at the Weizmann Institute of Science found a way around the missing date. Their paper appeared in *Nature Medicine* on 12 October 2026, and it changes the shape of the transition. At the final period, 52 of 90 laboratory tests jump. Not bend. Jump, in a step, inside a window of a year or two.

### The date nobody wrote down

The obstacle is bureaucratic before it is biological. No laboratory database records the day a woman's periods stop, so any curve plotted against age smears out. Two women born in the same year may be a decade apart in their hormone status, and averaging them erases the very moment you want to see.

Uri Alon and his colleagues borrowed a tool from astronomy. If you know how menopause ages are distributed in a population, you can treat that spread as a known distortion. Astronomers sharpen a blurred telescope image the same way. Subtract the smear and every test can be plotted against time to the final period instead of age.

They applied the method to two independent datasets. The first is NHANES, the American health survey that has run from 1999 to 2022. The second is the laboratory archive of Clalit, which insures roughly half of Israel and holds 1.3 million women aged 30 to 70 between 2004 and 2024. Together they hold more than 300 million lab tests. Ninety different tests went in, 57 of them present in both cohorts.

The data was already there. It sat in insurance records for two decades because nobody had asked it a question with a date in it.

### What jumped

Women showed a statistically significant jump in 52 of the 90 tests, measured against men of the same age spread. The biggest steps sit in bone metabolism, liver enzymes, lipid handling and osmolality, the measure of how the body balances fluid. Ferritin, an iron storage marker, rises by 0.6 standard deviations at the final period. For a curve built from a million people, that is a big step.

Men showed no jump at all. Run through the same algorithm with the same age distribution, their markers slide along a gentle slope. That comparison is what turns a statistical artefact into a physiological claim. An ageing effect would show up in both sexes. This one does not.

The American and Israeli cohorts agree, despite different lab vendors and different populations in two countries.

Some of the change moves in a welcome direction. Iron and haemoglobin improve after the final period, most likely because monthly blood loss stops.

### The hormones leave first

The reproductive hormones depart long before the cliff edge. According to the Nature press release, anti-Müllerian hormone falls sharply around 15 years before the final period. Follicle-stimulating hormone climbs around 14 years before it, and estradiol drops around 12 years before. For a woman whose last period lands at 50, the hormonal shift starts somewhere around 35 to 38.

That roughly doubles the old estimate. The paper cites the Study of Women's Health Across the Nation, known as SWAN, as the work it is arguing with. SWAN showed that sex hormone changes begin about seven years before menopause, on a few hundred women followed closely. The new reconstruction reaches back twice as far on far more people and far less detail per person.

A woman told to expect seven years of drift may dismiss what begins in her late thirties. That gap between seven and fifteen years is the practical core of the paper.

### Bone, heart and mood

The American survey also records diagnoses, and those follow the same shape. Osteoporosis prevalence picks up slope at the final period, then outpaces men. Heart attack and stroke accelerate after menopause too. Both match the laboratory signal in bone and lipid markers, which is what you want to see before you believe a finding.

Mood shows a spike and then a partial recovery. Moderate to severe depressive symptoms on the PHQ-9 scale reached 19% in the year after the final period. In the following 24 years the rate was 11%, with a p-value below 10⁻⁴. The denominators matter here: 46 of 236 women in that first year, against 698 of 6,495 later on. It is a real signal from a small group, reported as a transient rise.

Sleep behaves the same way. Difficulty falling asleep climbs after the transition and then settles back toward the male curve. The gap between men and women narrows in several places once the transition is done.

Bone loss and shifting lipids do not announce themselves. They are found, or they are not.

### Hormone therapy and the women who chose it

Women using menopausal hormone therapy showed markedly smaller jumps, particularly in bone, liver and kidney markers. Read plainly, that is an argument for starting treatment earlier than current practice does. Scientific American's coverage raises exactly that possibility, and adds that more research is needed first.

The paper is more careful than the headline version. Only about 9% of the American women in the dataset used hormones, mostly oral preparations, and they differ from the rest in ways no adjustment fully captures. Access to care, symptom severity and health behaviour all push in the same direction. The authors name healthy-user bias as a limitation themselves.

One detail deserves attention. Hormone therapy appeared to normalise most systems, yet it came with elevated CRP, a marker of inflammation. That fits an older finding about first-pass liver metabolism: oral estrogen raises CRP production, transdermal patches largely do not. Hormone therapy overall is anti-inflammatory, so the delivery route changes the lab picture.

### The window you may already be in

The practical takeaway concerns timing. If the hormonal phase begins in the mid-thirties and the visible cliff lands around 50, then the decade in which a baseline is most useful is the decade in which almost nobody orders one.

What that baseline looks like is not exotic: a lipid panel, a liver panel, a conversation about bone risk, and a depression screen. All of it is standard, cheap and available. The argument for doing it earlier comes from the shape of the data, not from a new test.

The uncomfortable part is arithmetic. By the time the cliff appears in a blood test, the shift behind it has been running for years, quietly, in a phase nobody had a reason to look at.

Two cautions belong next to that advice. No guideline changed because of this paper, and the authors call for prospective trials before the hormone therapy signal becomes clinical guidance. A population average also says nothing about one woman's timeline, and the spread between women in this data is wide.

Mary Claire Haver, the gynaecologist whose Instagram reel carried the study to a wide audience this week, makes the same point about her own practice. She does not wait for the last period before looking.

### What the method cannot tell you

This is a reconstruction from cross-sections, not a cohort that followed women through the transition. Instead of watching one person age, the authors pooled thousands of snapshots taken at different distances from the final period and let the algorithm put them in order.

The payoff is scale: 90 tests, two countries, a million women, 25 years. The cost is interpretation. Everything here describes the average path of a population. It cannot tell a 42-year-old what her own estradiol did last year, and it cannot tell a doctor when a specific patient crossed a threshold. The authors list retrospective data, self-reported menopause timing and the limits of deconvolution among their own caveats.

The causal question stays open as well. The link between hormone therapy and smaller jumps is consistent with a protective effect, and equally consistent with healthier women choosing treatment. Randomised trials settled a version of that question for bone two decades ago. They will be needed again for the wider picture.

The authors close by asking for the prospective study that follows individual women through the cliff. Until that exists, the sharpest picture of the transition sits in lab results that were already in an insurance database.
